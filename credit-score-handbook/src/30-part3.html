How Scores Are Calculated

The formulas are proprietary, but the ingredients are well known. Five factors explain almost everything.

No bureau publishes its full formula, and the models are re-estimated regularly. What the developers do publish is how much each category of information matters for the general population. Learn these weights and you will know where your effort pays off.

The five factors, as an underwriter sees them

1. Payment history: "Do you pay as agreed?"

This is the most important factor in every model. It covers whether you paid late, how late (30, 60 or 90+ days), how recently, and how often, plus any severe events: settlements, write-offs, collections, bankruptcy. One recent 30-day delinquency hurts more than several old ones. A 90-day default dominates a profile for years.

2. Amounts owed and utilisation: "How stretched are you?"

For revolving credit (cards, credit lines), the key measure is utilisation: reported balances divided by credit limits, both overall and per card. For instalment loans, models compare the remaining balance with the original amount; paying a loan down is a mildly positive signal. Utilisation has no memory in older models: bring it down and the score recovers by the next reporting cycle. That is why it is the quickest factor to fix (Chapter 12).

3. Length of history: "How long have we known you?"

This includes the age of your oldest account, the average age of all accounts, and how long since each was last used. You cannot speed it up. You can only avoid shortening it, which is why closing your oldest card is usually a mistake.

4. New credit and enquiries: "Are you suddenly hungry for credit?"

Several new accounts or hard enquiries in a short period are statistically linked to higher risk, because people in financial trouble often apply everywhere at once. The effect is modest and fades within months, but it can push a borderline application below a cut-off.

5. Credit mix: "Can you handle different kinds of debt?"

Having both revolving (cards) and instalment (loans) credit, and both secured and unsecured debt, is a slight positive. Never take a loan just to improve your mix. The interest will cost far more than the few points it might add.

The newer generation: trends and alternative data

  • Trended data. USA FICO 10T and VantageScore 4.0 look at about 24 months of balance history. A transactor who pays in full every month scores better than a revolver whose balance keeps rising, even when today's balances are the same.
  • Alternative data. USA Experian Boost (utility, phone, streaming and rent payments) and UltraFICO (bank-account cash-flow data) can help thin files. India The Account Aggregator framework lets lenders read your bank statements digitally with your consent. This makes cash-flow-based lending possible for borrowers with little bureau history.
  • Buy Now, Pay Later. USA FICO has released BNPL-aware versions of Score 10 and 10T, and some large BNPL providers now report to bureaus, but lenders are adopting them slowly. India Pay-later products from regulated lenders are already reported like any other loan.

Score Ranges, Bands and What Lenders Do With Them

The number matters less than which side of a lender's cut-off it falls on.

Every scoring system has a range and a set of bands. The bands published by score developers are only a guide. What actually decides your outcome are the internal cut-offs and pricing grids each lender sets. The scales below show the published or commonly used bands. The cliff effect: why 749 and 750 are different numbers

Lenders turn a continuous score into a small number of buckets, and they price by bucket. US conventional mortgage pricing adjustments step in roughly 20-point buckets (for example 760–779 and 780+). Many Indian banks publish home-loan rate cards with tiers such as 800+, 750–799 and 700–749. If your score is just below a breakpoint, a few points of improvement before you apply can move you into a cheaper tier for the entire loan term. Chapter 19 puts a price on it.

Do This Now

Before any large loan, ask the lender for its score tiers. If you are within about 20 points of the next tier, a 60–90 day sprint (pay card balances to under 10% before the statement dates, no new applications, fix report errors) often closes the gap.

When your scores disagree: a five-step diagnostic

  1. Label each score: the date, the bureau, and the model and version. Many apparent "drops" turn out to be comparisons between different products.
  2. Download the full reports behind both scores, from the same week if possible.
  3. Compare the account lists. An account that appears at one bureau but not another is the most common cause of a large gap.
  4. Compare balances and reporting dates. Differences in when utilisation was captured explain most moderate gaps.
  5. Compare negatives and enquiries. Anything negative that appears at only one bureau may be an error. Investigate it and dispute it if it is wrong.
Analyst's Note

Don't try to manage a score. Manage the data. Every scoring model reads the same raw facts: your payments, balances, ages and enquiries. Keep those clean and accurate and every score, from every bureau and every version, moves the same way. That is how I run my own credit, and it is the least stressful approach I know.

Checking your own score is free and harmless. Applying everywhere at once is neither.

India: enquiries carry extra weight in human underwriting

India Indian reports show each enquiry's purpose and amount. An underwriter who sees three personal-loan enquiries for ₹5 lakh within ten days, and no new loan, draws an obvious conclusion: you were turned down twice. Watch out for these traps:

  • "Check your eligibility" buttons on loan marketplaces that send your application to several lenders. Some create a hard enquiry with each one. Read the consent screen.
  • Agents filing on your behalf with five banks "to see who approves". Each one is an enquiry.
  • Re-applying straight after a rejection. Find the reason first.

Pre-approved and pre-qualified offers usually rely on soft data, and the lender makes a hard pull only when you accept and formally apply.

USA: the rate-shopping window

USA Scoring models recognise that shopping for the best mortgage, car loan or student loan is sensible behaviour:

  • FICO treats multiple mortgage, auto or student-loan enquiries within 45 days as one (older versions, still used in mortgages, use 14 days). It also ignores such enquiries made in the 30 days before scoring.
  • VantageScore groups enquiries made within a 14-day window.
  • Credit cards are not grouped. Every card application counts separately.

According to FICO, a single hard enquiry costs most people fewer than five points. The bigger risk is a cluster of new accounts, which also lowers your average account age.

The Analyst's Application Rules
  • Space card and loan applications at least 3–6 months apart unless you are rate-shopping a single loan.
  • Do all rate shopping for one loan within 14 days to be safe under every model.
  • Avoid any new credit applications for 6 months before a home loan.
  • After a rejection, get the reason (US lenders must send an adverse-action notice; in India, ask the lender) and fix it before applying again.
