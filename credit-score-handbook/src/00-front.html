Foreword

Why this book exists

For more than twenty-five years I have sat on the lending side of the table: approving and declining loans, building and testing scorecards, pricing risk, and dealing with what happens when the models get it wrong. One lesson has held throughout. Most people are never taught how credit works, yet the system keeps a detailed record of how they handle it and uses that record to price almost every loan they take.

I have seen doctors with seven-figure incomes turned down for home loans because of one ignored ₹800 annual fee on a card they had forgotten about. I have seen first-generation borrowers with modest salaries get the best rate a bank offers, because they had spent four quiet years paying every bill on time. A credit score does not measure wealth or intelligence. It measures behaviour, as the bureaus have recorded it. Behaviour can be learned, and recorded data can be checked and corrected.

This book sets out what I would tell a family member over a long cup of tea. It explains what a score really is, who the bureaus are and how they make money, how the numbers are calculated, why your scores differ, and how to build, repair and protect a credit profile. It then answers the practical question: which bureau, which score and which products suit which kind of borrower?

India and the United States get the most detailed treatment because their systems are the most commonly asked about and are well documented. A shorter chapter covers how credit reporting works elsewhere. The principles carry over to any country, even where the acronyms differ.

Read it once from start to finish, then keep it nearby. The chapters on disputes, persona playbooks and the action plan are meant to be used again and again.
