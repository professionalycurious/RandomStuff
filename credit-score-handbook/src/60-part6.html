Fifteen Myths, Retired

These come up again and again across the desk. Here is what is actually true.

01
Checking my own score lowers it.

Checking your own score is a soft enquiry. It is invisible to lenders and has no effect on your score. Check as often as you like.

02
I need to carry a balance and pay interest to build credit.

Paying the full statement balance builds exactly the same history. Interest is just a cost.

03
Closing old cards will improve my score.

It usually does the opposite. It removes available credit, which raises utilisation, and over time it shortens your history. Downgrade a card rather than closing it.

04
A higher salary means a higher score.

Income is not in your credit report. It affects how much you can borrow, not your score.

05
Using my debit card or UPI builds credit.

Neither is credit, so neither is reported to the bureaus. Only credit accounts build a credit history.

06
One late payment is no big deal.

For a high-score borrower, a single 30-day late can be one of the most damaging events. It stays visible for years: 36 months in India's payment grid and 7 years in the US.

07
Settling a loan is as good as closing it.

"Settled" means you paid less than you owed, and lenders read it that way for years. Pay in full and get the account marked "Closed" wherever you can.

08
Paying off a collection removes it.

USA It stays on the report, marked as paid. Newer models (FICO 9/10, VantageScore 3.0/4.0) ignore paid collections; FICO 8 does not.

09
Everyone has one credit score.

You have one per bureau, per model and per version, sometimes dozens. What matters is the data underneath them.

10
A paid service can remove negative items for me.

Accurate information cannot be removed by anyone before it expires. You can dispute inaccurate information yourself, for free.

11
Marriage merges our credit scores.

Credit files are individual. Only accounts you hold jointly appear on both files.

12
Never having borrowed means my credit is perfect.

No history usually means no score ("NA/NH"). Lenders cannot assess what they cannot see.

13
Paying the minimum due keeps me safe.

It avoids a late mark, but interest builds on the whole balance and high utilisation drags your score down. See the minimum-due maths in Chapter 12.

14
Being a guarantor doesn't affect me.

A guaranteed loan appears on your report as your liability. If the borrower defaults, it becomes your default too.

15
A freeze or monitoring service will hurt my score.

Neither affects your score. In the US a freeze is one of the best free protections available.

Five questions I am asked every week

"How long will it take to go from 650 to 750?"

If the low score comes from high utilisation, one to three reporting cycles. If it comes from recent late payments or a settlement, expect 12–24 months of spotless behaviour. There is no legitimate shortcut.

"Will prepaying and closing my loan lower my score?"

There can be a small, temporary dip because an active instalment account closes. That is never a reason to keep paying interest. The closed loan stays on your report as positive history.

"My co-applicant has a weak score. Does it matter?"

Yes. Lenders assess every applicant, and some price the loan on the weakest profile. The joint loan will also appear on both your reports from then on.

"Does a moratorium or restructuring hurt me?"

The account may be marked as restructured. Lenders read that in context, but it is still a signal of stress. Use relief only if you genuinely cannot pay.

"I have never missed a payment, so why am I stuck at 720?"

Usually because of high utilisation on one card, a short or young history, or several recent accounts or enquiries. Check utilisation card by card first.

Your Credit Action Plan

Knowledge only helps if you use it. Here is a plan for the next week, the next quarter and every year after.

This week

  • Download full reports from every bureau in your country.
  • Check personal details, account ownership, closures and enquiries.
  • Turn on autopay (at least the minimum, ideally the full statement balance) for every card and loan.
  • Note the statement and due dates for every card.
  • USA Freeze your credit at all three bureaus.

Within 90 days

  • Dispute every error, with evidence, at every bureau where it appears.
  • Bring total utilisation under 30%, and under 10% if you are borrowing soon.
  • Clear any small overdue amounts and forgotten annual fees.
  • India Start converting any "Settled" accounts to "Closed".
  • Ask for a limit increase on your oldest card if it won't trigger a hard enquiry.

Within 12 months

  • Reach a clean 12-month payment grid with no exceptions.
  • Keep a stable set of 2–3 cards; downgrade unused fee cards instead of closing them.
  • Use a secured card if you are building or rebuilding.
  • Make no unnecessary applications. Space applications 3–6 months apart.
  • Check your score trend each quarter from the same source.

Every year

  • India Take free reports from all four CICs, staggered by quarter.
  • Review any guarantor or joint liabilities you have signed.
  • Update your mobile number and e-mail with every lender so alerts reach you.
  • Re-check your home-loan spread against your current score.
  • Make sure nothing on a card you no longer use is quietly falling overdue.

The 90-day pre-loan sprint

WhenActions
Day 90Pull all reports. List errors and file disputes. Stop all new credit applications. Ask lenders about their score tiers.
Day 60Check dispute results. Pay card balances down to under 10% before each statement date. Clear any overdues.
Day 30Re-check every report. Confirm corrections and low reported balances. Gather NOCs and evidence for the underwriter.
Day 0Apply to your shortlisted lenders within a short window (14 days). Compare total cost, not just the headline rate.

The analyst's ten commandments

  1. Never pay late. Use autopay as a safety net.
  2. Keep reported balances low: under 30% always, under 10% when it matters.
  3. Pay in full. Rewards never make up for interest.
  4. Keep your oldest accounts open and occasionally used.
  5. Apply rarely and on purpose. Every application leaves a record.
  6. Read the reports, not just the score, from every bureau.
  7. Dispute errors at once, with evidence, everywhere they appear.
  8. Close debts fully. Avoid "Settled" if you can.
  9. Guard your identity: freezes, alerts, no shared OTPs.
  10. Think in rupees and dollars. Every on-time payment lowers the cost of your next loan.

A final word from the desk

In twenty-five years I have watched scoring models change, regulators rewrite the rules, and reporting speed up from quarterly to monthly to weekly. Through all of it, the borrowers who did best were not the cleverest or the richest. They were the most consistent. They paid on time, borrowed for a reason, read their reports, and fixed errors as soon as they found them. The system rewards that steadiness more reliably than anything else. I hope this book helps you build it.

A–Z

Glossary

The vocabulary of credit, in plain language.

Account Aggregator (AA)
India's RBI-regulated, consent-based framework for sharing financial data, such as bank statements, with lenders.
Adverse action notice
US notice from a lender explaining a decline or worse terms, naming the bureau it used.
Authorised user
A person added to someone else's card. In the US the account usually appears on their report.
Charge-off / Write-off
The lender records the debt as a loss. It is still owed and is severely negative.
CIC
Credit Information Company: India's term for a credit bureau, licensed by the RBI under CICRA 2005.
CIR
Credit Information Report: the Indian term for a credit report.
CKYC number
Central KYC identifier, now included in Indian credit reporting to improve identity matching.
CMR (CIBIL MSME Rank)
Business credit rank from 1 (lowest risk) to 10, used for MSME lending.
Collection
A debt passed to a collection agency. In the US it is reported as a separate entry.
CRA
Consumer Reporting Agency: the US legal term for a credit bureau.
Credit freeze
A US tool that blocks new creditors from accessing your report. It is free and does not affect your score.
Credit mix
The variety of credit types you hold, such as revolving and instalment, secured and unsecured.
DPD
Days Past Due: how late a payment was when it was reported.
DTI / FOIR
Debt-to-Income or Fixed Obligations to Income Ratio: the share of income already committed to debt payments.
EBLR
External Benchmark Lending Rate. Indian floating retail loans are priced as a benchmark (usually repo) plus a spread.
FCRA
Fair Credit Reporting Act (1970): the main US law on credit reports.
FICO Score
The most widely used US scoring family. Base range 300–850; industry versions 250–900.
Fraud alert
A US flag asking lenders to verify identity before granting credit. Lasts 1 or 7 years.
Furnisher
A lender or other entity that reports data to the bureaus.
Hard enquiry
A credit check triggered by an application. Visible to lenders, with a small, temporary effect on your score.
Instalment credit
A loan with fixed repayments and an end date, such as a home, car or personal loan.
LLPA
Loan-Level Price Adjustment: US mortgage pricing add-ons that vary by credit score and LTV.
LTV
Loan-to-Value: the loan amount divided by the value of the collateral.
NA / NH
Not Applicable / No History: Indian bureaus' label for a file too thin to score.
NOC / No Dues Certificate
A lender's written confirmation that a loan has been fully repaid. Keep it permanently.
NPA
Non-Performing Asset: in India, a loan generally overdue for more than 90 days.
Revolving credit
A credit line you can reuse up to a limit, such as a credit card or overdraft.
Risk-based pricing
Charging different rates or fees depending on predicted risk, mostly driven by your score.
Settled
An account closed by paying less than the full amount owed. Negative for years.
SMA
Special Mention Account (India). SMA-0, 1 and 2 mean overdue 1–30, 31–60 and 61–90 days.
Soft enquiry
A check that does not affect your score, such as your own check, a pre-approval or an account review.
Thin file
A credit file with too little history for a reliable score.
Tri-merge
A US mortgage credit report combining data from all three bureaus.
Trended data
Balance and payment patterns over time (about 24 months), used by FICO 10T and VantageScore 4.0.
Utilisation
Revolving balances divided by revolving limits, overall and per card.
VantageScore
Scoring model owned jointly by the three US bureaus. Range 300–850.
